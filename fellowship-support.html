Summary and research overview

My research focuses on parallel algorithms and software for sparse matrices and graphs, with applications in scientific computing, biology, and machine learning. A central problem is how to distribute data and computation across processors while keeping communication costs low.

The work described below falls into three areas:

  • Sparse algorithms: hypersparse matrix representations, parallel sparse matrix products, and graph algorithms expressed through linear algebra.
  • Software and standards: Combinatorial BLAS and the GraphBLAS C API.
  • Computational biology: HipMCL and PASTIS, which we used for large-scale protein-family discovery.

Since November 2025, I have been a Senior Libraries Engineer at NVIDIA. Before that, I held appointments as a Senior Scientist at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and an Adjunct Associate Professor in UC Berkeley EECS. I earned my Ph.D. at UC Santa Barbara in 2010, advised by John R. Gilbert, and my undergraduate degree at Sabancı University in 2005.

Research highlights

Parallel sparse computation

With John Gilbert, I studied how to represent and multiply sparse matrices on distributed-memory systems. When a matrix is split across many processors, a local block can contain very few nonzeros relative to its dimensions. Our work on hypersparse matrices addressed the storage and computational overhead in this case.

I also worked on parallel sparse matrix-matrix multiplication and indexing, compressed sparse blocks for matrix-vector and transpose-vector multiplication, and algorithms that exploit multiple levels of parallelism.

Selected papers

  • Parallel sparse matrix-vector and matrix-transpose-vector multiplication using compressed sparse blocks — SPAA, 2009.
  • Parallel sparse matrix-matrix multiplication and indexing: Implementation and experiments — SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing, 2012.
  • Exploiting multiple levels of parallelism in sparse matrix-matrix multiplication — SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing, 2016.

GraphBLAS and reusable software

The Combinatorial BLAS implements graph algorithms using distributed-memory sparse linear algebra. I worked on its design, implementation, and applications. Its operations include sparse matrix-matrix and matrix-vector multiplication.

I was one of the original five members of the GraphBLAS C API team and made substantial technical contributions to the interface and specification. I also helped guide the broader GraphBLAS effort as a steering committee member.

GraphBLAS gives graph algorithms a common set of linear algebra operations, so algorithm developers can use a standard interface rather than write against a particular implementation.

From sparse algorithms to protein-family discovery

I conceived and led the design of HipMCL and PASTIS. HipMCL is a scalable parallel implementation of Markov clustering; PASTIS uses sparse matrix operations for distributed-memory many-to-many protein sequence comparison.

Together, these tools helped us more than double the number of known protein families in the global metagenomics study published in Nature in 2023. I was also the team leader for the 2022 ACM Gordon Bell Prize finalist work on extreme-scale many-against-many protein similarity search.

Papers and performance
  • HipMCL: A high-performance parallel implementation of the Markov clustering algorithm for large-scale networks — Nucleic Acids Research, 2018.
  • Unraveling the functional dark matter through global metagenomics — Nature, 2023.
  • Extreme-scale many-against-many protein similarity search — SC, 2022; longer CV, p. 6.
  • HipMCL clustered a network with 75 million nodes and 68 billion edges in 2.4 hours using 2,000 nodes of NERSC’s Cori system. Software record, p. 20.

I also contributed to HipMer/MetaHipMer genome assembly and BELLA long-read alignment.

Machine learning and randomized methods

With my students and collaborators, I developed CAGNET, a family of distributed graph neural network training algorithms. CAGNET uses different decompositions of sparse-dense matrix multiplication to reduce communication. We also worked on distributed sampling for graph neural network training, randomized sparse tensor decomposition, and leverage-score sampling.

Selected papers and software
  • Reducing communication in graph neural network training — SC, 2020.
  • Distributed matrix-based sampling for graph neural network training — MLSys, 2024; longer CV, p. 5.
  • Fast exact leverage score sampling from Khatri-Rao products with applications to tensor decomposition — NeurIPS, 2023; and Distributed-memory randomized algorithms for sparse tensor CP decomposition — SPAA, 2024. Longer CV, p. 5.
  • CAGNET software description, p. 20.

GPU computing and current work

My GPU work includes path algorithms, sparse matrix multiplication, and GraphBLAST, developed with Carl Yang and John D. Owens. At NVIDIA, I primarily work on our next-generation iterative solvers (cuISS), but I also frequently collaborate with cuDSS and cuSPARSE teams.

Our forthcoming SC26 paper, Tackling Parallelization Challenges of Randomized Preconditioners with Dependency Tracking, introduces ParAC, which uses dynamic dependency tracking to parallelize randomized approximate Cholesky preconditioning on CPUs and GPUs.

Related papers: GraphBLAST, ACM TOMS, 2022; GPU sparse multiplication, Euro-Par, 2018; GPU path problems, Parallel Computing, 2010.

Teaching and curriculum development

I co-taught CS267: Applications of Parallel Computers at UC Berkeley in each spring from 2017 through 2025, with Jim Demmel, Katherine Yelick, and Laura Grigori across different offerings.

I modernized the course with multiple lectures on parallel algorithms for machine learning and expanded its coverage of distributed-memory programming, graph algorithms, sorting and searching, and computational biology.

I also advised master’s students, undergraduate researchers, and summer interns, and served on other students’ Ph.D. committees.

For the letter writer

SIAM asks recommenders to explain how they know the candidate, assess the significance of the contributions in their own voice, and supply concrete evidence. The committee need not be expert in the candidate’s subfield. A focused account of a few contributions is more useful than a long publication list. Official letter guidance.

For the 2027 Fellows cycle, nominations close October 15, 2026. Each recommendation letter is limited to two pages, using an allowed font, US-letter paper, one-inch margins, and at most six lines per inch. The package requires exactly two recommendation letters, separate from the nomination letter; recommenders cannot also be nominators. Please coordinate submission with the nominators and check the current SIAM procedures for the complete rules.

Please focus on the work you know firsthand: the problem we addressed, my contribution, and how you assess its importance.
